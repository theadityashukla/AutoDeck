# Slide design

You are designing within a client's slide master: its fonts, its palette, its margins.
Inside that, every decision is yours. The aim is a deck a senior reader trusts at a
glance: calm, consistent, and built so the eye lands on the evidence for each header.

## 1. The story decides the slide

Design after the storyline, never before. Each slide exists to make its header believable,
so ask first: what is the one thing on this slide that proves the header? That is the
focal point, and the layout is built around it. A slide whose design you cannot explain in
terms of its header is decoration.

## 2. One system per deck

Pick the system on the first content slide and keep it on every slide that follows:

- the same margins and header position;
- the same grid (two halves, thirds, or a 12-column split);
- the same roles for the same jobs: one size for headers, one for body, one for labels;
- one accent colour, meaning "look here", and nothing else.

Vary the *layout* from slide to slide, so a chart slide and a number slide look different,
but never the system. A reader should feel the deck was made by one hand. Changing
backgrounds, fonts or accent from slide to slide breaks the system, however good each
slide looks alone.

## 3. Restraint

Every shape must do a job: group things that belong together, separate things that do
not, mark the focal point, or carry data. If removing a shape loses nothing, remove it.

Never:

- ornaments with no information: decorative circles, pill shapes, giant punctuation,
  rotated cards, background graphics that echo the subject;
- full-bleed colour blocks for their own sake. A coloured field is for one deliberate
  moment, such as the title or the closing ask, not a pattern;
- several accents on one slide, or the accent used for decoration;
- tilted, skewed or overlapping-for-effect elements.

A slide that would work as a poster, a slogan in huge type over a colour field, is usually
a bad deck slide. It asks the reader to trust a claim without showing them the evidence.

## 4. Few words

The point should be carried by the evidence itself: the chart with the focal bars in the
accent, the one number, the diagram. A slide does not need to be text-heavy to make its
point. Body text explains what the visual cannot: the population, the comparator, the
condition. It does not repeat the header in other words.

Good proportions for a presented slide: a header, one visual or number, and one to three
short supporting lines. A read-ahead deck can carry more, in a clear reading order.

## 5. One focal point

Make the evidence for the header the largest, highest-contrast or only coloured thing on
the slide. Use a chart's `emphasis` to put the categories the header talks about in the
accent and leave the rest neutral. If two things compete for the eye, one of them belongs
on another slide.

## 6. Chrome is a choice

A section tracker, page numbers, stickers ("Preliminary", "Illustrative") and numbered
footnotes help a deck that is **read**: a pre-read, a long committee pack, something
printed and annotated. A deck that is **presented** to a small audience usually needs none
of it. Decide once for the deck. If you use chrome, put it in the same place on every
slide, in the label role, out of the way. Every chrome word is a block too.

## 7. Space, alignment and size

- Leave real space around the focal point. Empty space is not wasted; it is what makes the
  focal point read as focal.
- Align edges. Text frames on a slide share left edges or centre lines; a frame that is
  nearly aligned looks like a mistake.
- Size by importance: the header, then the focal evidence, then support, then labels. A
  big number may be very big, because it is the point. Set `size_pt` for it and label what
  it measures right beside it.
- Give text frames room: a frame that only just fits will fail the fit check after a small
  edit.

## 8. Layout patterns: vocabulary, not templates

These are moves you can make, not slides to copy. Combine and adapt them.

- **Chart and takeaways:** the chart across about two thirds, with two or three numbered
  so-whats beside it.
- **The number:** one figure dominates, its label and its context beside it.
- **Columns:** two to four parallel items with a shared structure, each headed.
- **Before and after:** two states side by side, the change between them marked.
- **Process:** a short sequence of steps in a strip, the critical step marked.
- **Question and answer:** a question header, and the evidence that answers it.
- **Table:** rows of options against criteria, the winning cells marked in the accent.

## 9. Accessibility

Contrast at least 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and chart marks. Never let
colour carry meaning alone: pair it with a label, a position or weight. Respect the
master's minimum type size.

## Checking your own slide

Before you return a layout, read each slide as the audience will:

1. Does the eye land on the evidence for the header within a second?
2. Is the system the same as the slide before (margins, header position, accent)?
3. Is there any shape that does no job? Remove it.
4. Is there any sentence that repeats the header? Cut it.
5. Would a senior reader find it calm and credible, or loud?
