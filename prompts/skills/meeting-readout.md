# Meeting readout

A readout tells people who were not in the meeting what it means for them. It is not the
notes on slides. The notes are complete; the readout is selective, and argued.

## Find the governing thought

Ask what the audience must know or do because this meeting happened. Usually one of:
- **A decision was made** and they need to act on it or plan around it.
- **A decision is needed** and the meeting framed it; the readout asks for it.
- **Something changed** (a date moved, a risk appeared) and they need to adjust.

Lead with that, answer-first. The storyline skill's ghost deck still applies: every headline
in order should read as the argument. A readout of a short meeting is often three to five
slides; resist one slide per agenda item.

## What becomes a slide

- Decisions and changes that affect the audience: a slide each, or one slide for several
  small ones.
- Action items: one slide, with owners as the transcript gives them and "Owner TBD" where
  it does not. Never fill an owner to make the slide look finished.
- Key dates: a timeline only if there are several and their order matters; keep each date at
  the precision it was said.
- Open questions and risks: what the audience can help settle, as the closing ask.
- Themes rarely become slides on their own. They are context for a decision.

## Quoting a meeting

Cite the turns, as the `citations` skill describes for transcripts: verbatim, mistakes
included, the claim spelled right. The audit shows each citation as "from the meeting" with
its time. Keep the speaker's hedge in the claim: "the team aims to launch on the sixteenth",
not "launch is on the 16th". Attribute a statement to a person only when the transcript
shows who said it.

## Written for people who were not there

Say who met and what for, in a line, on the first slide or in its notes. Spell out what the
room took for granted: names of systems, acronyms, who "she" is. Nobody in the audience
heard the conversation; every claim must make sense on its own.
