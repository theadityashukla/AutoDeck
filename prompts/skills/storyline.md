# Storyline

Decide the argument before you build a single slide. A deck whose slides are individually
good but whose sequence argues nothing is a pile of facts, and the reader has to do the
synthesis you were asked to do.

## 1. The governing thought

The governing thought is the one sentence the whole deck argues: what the audience should
conclude, or decide, once they have seen it all. Everything else in the deck is a reason, a
piece of evidence, or a step supporting it.

- Write it as a full sentence that could be disagreed with. *"Drug A is a credible
  first injection-free option for adults already on metformin, within the trial
  populations"* is a governing thought. *"Drug A review"* is a topic.
- It must be **entailed by the evidence as written.** It is the deck's highest-risk sentence:
  every slide inherits its strength. If the strongest version is not supported, write the
  version that is.
- It appears, in some form, on the title slide (as the title), on the first content slide,
  and on the closing slide. On a long deck it returns at each section boundary: each section
  should visibly link back to it. Repeat the *idea*, not the identical sentence.

### Answer-first or question-led — you decide, and you say which

**Answer-first** (the default): when the evidence supports one governing thought, state the
answer at the start and spend the rest of the deck proving it. Executives read the first
slides and skim the rest; give them the answer where they will see it.

**Question-led**: when the evidence does *not* support a single answer — sources disagree,
the key measurement was never made, the result holds for one group and not another — then
forcing an answer is overgeneralisation at the scale of the whole deck. Lead with the
question the audience is asking, lay out what the evidence shows and what it does not show,
and close with what would settle it and what can be decided now.

> Answer-first title: *"Drug A matched or beat every active comparator on HbA1c"*
> Question-led title: *"Can a tablet replace the first injection? What four trials show,
> and what they leave open"*

Choose deliberately. Say which you chose, and why, in the draft's `notes`.

## 2. The opening: situation, complication, question, answer

The first two or three content slides set up why the deck matters, in this order:

- **Situation** — what the audience already accepts. Brief; no news here.
- **Complication** — what changed, what is in the way, or what is unknown. This is what makes
  the deck necessary.
- **Question** — the question the complication raises, as the audience would ask it.
- **Answer** — the governing thought (answer-first), or the promise of what the deck will
  establish (question-led).

On a short deck these compress into one or two slides. On a deck of any length, the opening
should make an **empowerment promise**: say plainly what the reader will be able to decide,
or know, by the end. *"By the end of this deck you can choose between the tablet and the
injectable for the next formulary review"* — expressed as the slide's point, not as a
greeting. Then the closing slide delivers on that promise, point for point.

## 3. The body: a pyramid under the governing thought

Under the governing thought sit three to five **key-line claims**: the reasons it is true, or
the steps to act on it. Under each key line sit the slides that establish it.

- **Group without overlap and without gaps.** Sibling points should not double-count (if one
  is "labour costs" another cannot be "overtime"), and together they should cover what the
  parent claims. Do this sorting in your head, as structure. Do not print the categories as
  slide titles: a slide's headline states the insight from its group (*"Labour costs have
  stabilised at the 2023 level"*), not the group's name (*"Labour analysis"*).
- **Never claim completeness you cannot show.** A headline that says "the three reasons" needs
  evidence there are three. Usually "three reasons" is enough.
- **Order sections by what the reader needs first** to accept what comes next: context before
  evidence, evidence before implication, implication before recommendation.
- **Each slide answers "why?" or "how?" about the one above it** in the pyramid. A slide that
  answers neither does not belong in the body.

## 4. The ghost deck: headlines first, then test them

Before writing any body, write every slide's headline in order (see `headlines`). This list is
the ghost deck. Then test it:

1. **The paragraph test.** Read the headlines top to bottom as if they were the only text in
   the deck. They should read as a connected argument: someone who sees only the headlines
   knows what you concluded, why, and what to do. If they read like a table of contents
   (*"Background", "Market analysis", "Conclusions"*), the storyline has failed.
2. **The duplicate test.** Two headlines that say the same thing are one slide.
3. **The gap test.** If the argument jumps (from "the drug works" straight to "adopt it", with
   nothing about safety or cost), a slide is missing, or the jump becomes a stated limitation.
4. **The entailment test.** For each headline, can you point to the evidence that will carry
   it? If not, weaken it, re-scope it, turn it into a question, or remove it, *now*.
5. **The transition test.** Each headline should follow from the one before, even if only by
   contrast (*"…but the benefit narrows in patients over 65"*).

## 5. Narrative roles

Every slide gets a `narrative_role`: its job in the argument.

| Role | The slide's job |
|---|---|
| `title` | Opens the deck; states its answer or its question |
| `agenda` | Shows the route (long decks only) |
| `context` | What the audience needs to know to follow the argument (situation) |
| `problem` | What is in the way, unknown, or at stake (complication) |
| `evidence` | Data that establishes a point |
| `comparison` | Options or results side by side |
| `mechanism` | How or why something works |
| `implication` | What the evidence means for this audience |
| `recommendation` | The decision or action proposed |
| `risk` | Limitations, open questions, what could change the answer |
| `next_steps` | What happens after the meeting |
| `appendix` | Supporting detail kept out of the main argument |

A typical findings deck runs `title → context → problem → evidence… → implication →
recommendation → next_steps`. Put `risk` next to the claim it qualifies, or just before the
recommendation. A slide you cannot give a role to usually does not need to exist.

Every slide also gets an `intent`: one line saying what it must accomplish (*"establish that
weight loss matched, not beat, comparator B"*), never a topic label (*"weight slide"*).

## 6. Structure slides

- **Title slide.** The title states the deck's answer, or its question, in a sentence a
  sponsor could repeat. The subtitle names the audience or the occasion. Nothing on it is a
  claim.
- **Agenda and section dividers only for decks of 8 or more slides.** A divider only opens a
  section of at least two slides, and only where the deck has at least two sections. A
  six-slide deck needs neither: its headlines are its agenda. Never write a section number;
  code numbers dividers. When an agenda is used, each divider repeats an agenda item's wording
  exactly, so the reader can find their place.
- **No "thank you", "questions?", "overview" or "summary" slides.** They waste the slot that
  stays on screen longest.

## 7. The close: contributions and the ask

The last content slide is not a summary of the past. It says what the deck has **established**
and what the audience is **asked to do**:

- two or more established points, each already made and cited earlier in the deck, stated
  no more strongly than they were there; and
- one explicit ask or decision: approve, choose, fund, test, or what to do next.

Write the close with forward-looking verbs (*approve, choose, start, test, allocate*). If the
deck is question-led, the close says what can be decided now and what evidence would settle
the rest. The closing slide answers the empowerment promise from the opening.

## 8. Length, body and appendix

- **Size the deck to the argument,** not to a number. Most decisions need 6 to 12 slides. If
  the person asked for a length, meet it; if the honest argument needs fewer slides, say so in
  `notes` rather than padding.
- **The body holds what the decision rests on.** Methods, sensitivity analyses, full tables
  and secondary results go to `appendix` slides when they are needed at all.
- **Hedges never go to the appendix.** A qualifier that limits a body claim stays on the body
  slide, next to the claim. The appendix may hold *more* detail about a limitation; it never
  holds the limitation *instead of* the body slide.

## 9. Pre-read or presented

Most decks this system writes are **read without a presenter**: sent ahead, read on a laptop,
forwarded. Such a slide must carry its own argument: a full-sentence headline, enough body to
prove it, and its qualifiers visible. Speaker notes are not seen by most readers.

If the brief says the deck will be **presented**, the body can be lighter and the notes carry
the talk track. They complement the slide rather than repeating it. Even then, a limit on a
claim lives on the slide, not only in the notes.

## 10. Advice to leave out

Some well-known presentation advice comes from live talks and does not apply here: near-empty
slides with a picture and no words, deleting slide titles, "no more than three bullets",
"ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point font". A pre-read deck needs assertive headlines and
enough body to stand alone. What carries over is the discipline behind that advice: one idea
per slide, no decoration, and no slide that a presenter would have to read aloud to make sense
of.

## Worked example — a ghost deck that fails, and the fix

Brief: a medical-affairs team deciding whether to brief payers on drug A, a fictional
once-daily tablet.

**Failing ghost deck** (topic labels; reads like a table of contents, as live run 1 did):

1. Drug A in type 2 diabetes
2. Glycemic control: HbA1c reduction
3. Body weight reduction across ALPHA trials
4. Safety and tolerability
5. Conclusions

**Fixed ghost deck** (answer-first, each line arguable and carried by evidence):

1. *(title)* Drug A is a credible injection-free first step, within trial populations
2. *(problem)* Patients who need more than metformin often delay because the next step is an
   injection
3. *(evidence)* Drug A lowered HbA1c more than comparators B and C at 24 weeks
4. *(comparison)* Weight loss beat comparator C but only matched comparator B
5. *(risk)* Side effects were mostly gastrointestinal and eased over the first weeks
6. *(recommendation)* Brief payers on drug A as a first tablet step for metformin-treated
   adults

Read the right-hand list alone: it is an argument. Slide 4 holds back ("only matched")
because the evidence did. Slide 6 asks for something specific.
