# Writing style

Slide prose is consulting prose: definite, plain, specific. Every word either carries
information or goes. The reader should never have to wonder what a sentence actually
claims.

## Write like this

- **Nouns, verbs and numbers.** Name the thing, say what it did, give the figure with its
  condition. *"Changeover time fell from 42 to 31 minutes on the two pilot lines."*
- **Specific over general.** *"Three of five regional sites missed their throughput target"*,
  not *"several sites faced challenges"*.
- **Active voice, with the actor named,** where the source names one. *"The authors report…"*,
  *"The trial measured…"*.
- **Short sentences.** One claim per sentence, one sentence per bullet.
- **Sentence case** for headlines and labels. No final period on headlines or one-line
  bullets.
- **Parallel lists.** Items in one list share a grammatical shape, without all starting with
  the same word.

## Cut these

These are the marks of machine-written text. They add length, hide the claim, and make an
expert reader stop trusting the slide.

| Tic | Example | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| Throat-clearing opener | "In today's rapidly evolving landscape, organisations face unprecedented challenges" | Start with the finding: "Three of five regional sites missed their 2025 throughput target" |
| Inflated adjectives | "a robust, transformative solution delivering significant value" | Say what it did: "cut changeover time from 42 to 31 minutes on two pilot lines" |
| Stock verbs | delve, leverage, unlock, foster, harness, empower, streamline, revolutionise | Use the plain verb: use, open, build, cut, raise, allow |
| Stock nouns | landscape, synergy, paradigm, journey, ecosystem, game-changer | Name the actual thing |
| "It is important to note that…" | "It is worth noting that weight loss was similar" | "Weight loss was similar" |
| "Not just X, but Y" | "Not just a tablet, but a new paradigm" | Say what it is |
| Vague quantifiers | many, numerous, significant, substantial, a wide range | The number, cited, or nothing |
| Uniform gerund bullets | "Driving growth…", "Enhancing efficiency…", "Fostering innovation…" | Subject–verb statements of fact |
| Symmetric triads | Three bullets or pillars on every slide whatever the evidence holds | As many items as the evidence has, two to six |
| Intensifiers | very, highly, extremely, truly, crucially, notably | Delete |
| Empty conclusions | "This highlights the importance of…", "This underscores…" | Say what follows, or nothing |

## One name per concept

Naming a thing gives the reader a handle on it, but only if the name stays the same. Once a
concept has a name on a slide, **use exactly that name everywhere**: headlines, bodies,
labels, notes.

- Do not vary it for elegance. If slide 2 says *"drug A"*, slide 5 does not say *"the oral
  agent"*, *"the tablet formulation"* and the brand name in turn. Pick one; if a second name
  is needed (brand vs generic), introduce it once (*"drug A (brand name)"*) and then use one
  consistently.
- When the deck coins a term for its central idea (a strategy, a mechanism, a segment), define
  it once, early, and use it verbatim after that.
- Keep the source's name for a measured quantity. If it says "HbA1c", do not switch to "blood
  sugar" on one slide and "glycaemic control" on another, unless you define the plain-word
  version once for a lay audience and stick to it.
- Project-specific names that contain digits (*ALPHA-7, GLP-1, int8*) are names, copied
  exactly as the source writes them.

## Acronyms and jargon

- **Define an acronym at its first use** unless the audience description says they use it
  daily. A payer audience knows HbA1c; a board may not know KV cache.
- Once defined, use the acronym consistently.
- Replace jargon with the audience's word when the two mean the same. Keep a term of art when
  the audience uses it, or when the plain word would be less precise.

## Numbers in text

- Copy the number exactly as the cited passage has it. Do not round, convert or re-express
  (no "about a third" for 31%), unless the conversion is a declared derivation.
- Units every time: *"1.3 percentage points"* vs *"1.3%"* are different claims. Say which the
  source means.
- Keep the source's precision. If it says 29 ms, do not write "about 30 ms".
