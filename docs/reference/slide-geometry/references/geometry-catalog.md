# Geometry catalog

Indexed two ways: by point count (the common entry) and by relationship (for any count). Each entry gives: when to use it, the form, and label placement. Construction math lives in `construction.md`.

Selection tiebreakers when several fit: prefer the geometry the audience can parse in two seconds; prefer horizontal reading flows for formal audiences; never repeat the previous slide's geometry; when a slide's points carry numbers, prefer the geometry whose proportions can honestly encode them.

## Two points

**Split canvas (diagonal).** Complement or contrast between two coequal ideas. Divide the slide with a diagonal (not vertical) line — the diagonal reads as designed, the vertical as default. One half may be tinted or inverted (dark/light). Labels: a short title in the upper area of each half, supporting line beneath; keep both halves' text blocks aligned to the same baselines.

**Balance beam.** Tradeoff — more of one means less of the other. A horizontal beam on a small triangular fulcrum, one labeled weight at each end; tilt the beam a few degrees only if the slide's argument is that one side outweighs the other (proportion is a claim). Labels inside or directly above the weights; the tension being traded (cost vs. speed) captioned under the fulcrum.

**Interlocking halves.** Two parts completing one whole (strategy + execution). Two arcs or two offset semicircle segments that visibly form a single circle, with a slight gap or overlap at the joins. Labels centered in each half; the whole they form captioned beneath the circle.

**Shared core.** Two entities with a common foundation. Two circles or panels connected to a single smaller shape between/beneath them. Labels in each outer shape; the shared element labeled inside the core.

**Crossing arrow (before → after).** Transformation. Left state and right state as two simple framed areas, one bold arrow crossing the divide; the arrow may carry a 1–3 word label naming the intervention. Old state visually quieter (muted tint), new state carries the accent.

**Opposed arrows.** Direct opposition or competing forces. Two arrows pointing at each other with a narrow gap; the contested ground labeled in the gap. Use sparingly — genuinely adversarial content only.

**Gap and bridge.** Current vs. target with an obstacle between. Two elevated platforms with a visible gap; a bridge element labeled with the capability that closes it. Strong for consulting "how we get you there" slides.

## Three points

**Triangle, vertices.** Three coequal elements of one system. Equilateral triangle, point-up; elements at the vertices as small circles or nodes. Labels *outside* each vertex: top label centered above, bottom-left label right-aligned to the left of its node, bottom-right label left-aligned to the right. Optional: the system they compose named in the triangle's center.

**Triangle, edge arrows.** Three mutually reinforcing elements — each strengthens the next. As above, plus a curved arrow along each edge, all running the same rotational direction. This is the canonical upgrade from "three boxes": the arrows *are* the claim of reinforcement. If reinforcement is mutual in both directions, use double-headed edge arcs, but prefer one direction — it reads faster.

**Three-node cycle.** Repeating three-phase loop. Three nodes at 120° spacing on an invisible circle, connected by arc arrows head-to-tail. Differs from the triangle in that the *process repeats*; if it does not repeat, it is a sequence — use a path.

**Converging arrows.** Three inputs producing one outcome. Three arrows from the left/top meeting at a single labeled result shape. Inputs labeled at the arrow tails.

**Podium.** Three ranked items. Three columns of honestly proportioned heights, center-tallest optional; labels above each column, rank or metric inside.

**Radial trio.** Three supports around one center. Central shape holding the goal; three satellites at 120° with plain connector lines (not arrows — no flow is claimed). Satellite labels placed outside, away from center.

## Four points

**True 2×2.** Four positions defined by two continuums. Two axis lines with arrowheads, each axis labeled at both ends with the poles of a *real* continuum (low→high cost, familiar→novel). Items plotted as labeled dots or small shapes at their honest positions; quadrants may carry quiet background tints and a corner caption naming each regime. If you cannot name both axes as continuums, this is not a 2×2 — reclassify.

**Compass.** Four directions, options, or dimensions around one center. Center names the subject; four satellites at N/E/S/W with plain connectors. Reads calmer than a 2×2 and claims no axes.

**Square cycle.** Four-phase repeating loop. Four nodes at the corners of an invisible rounded square, arrows head-to-tail clockwise. Start node carries the accent color.

**Four strata.** Four layers of a stack or foundation. Horizontal bands, foundation at the bottom widest if the argument is "everything rests on this"; equal bands if layers are peers. Labels left-aligned inside each band, one supporting line maximum.

**Two-by-two split canvas.** Four items that are really two pairs. Diagonal or cross split into four regions with the pairing made visible (matching tints across the diagonal). Use when a plain grid would hide the pairing.

## Five to six points

**Radial hub.** Five or six elements supporting one center. Satellites evenly spaced on a circle (72° / 60°), plain connectors, labels placed outside each satellite on its radial line — never overlapping a neighbor. The strongest replacement for "six boxes in a 3×2 grid."

**Ring cycle.** Five or six repeating phases. Arc segments forming a ring with small gaps, arrowheads on each segment's end; labels outside the ring at each segment's angular center, connected by a short leader line if needed.

**Journey path.** Five or six sequential milestones. A gently curved path (one or two bends) traversing the slide left to right, milestone nodes along it, labels alternating above/below the path to avoid crowding. Number the nodes.

**Funnel.** Five or six narrowing stages. Stacked trapezoids of honestly decreasing width; stage labels inside each band, metrics (counts, conversion) right-aligned beside the funnel. If widths do not reflect real quantities, keep the taper subtle and uniform.

**Staircase.** Five or six cumulative steps toward a summit. Ascending steps left to right; labels on each tread; the goal labeled at the top. Claims *cumulative progress* — each step retained — unlike a path, which claims only order.

## Any count, by relationship

**Pyramid / strata** (hierarchy, foundation): horizontal layers, apex = fewest/most refined. Layer labels inside; never 3D.
**Iceberg** (visible vs. hidden): a waterline across the slide; the small visible portion above, the large hidden mass below; labels on each side of the line. Powerful for effort/cost slides.
**Tree / delta** (divergence): one root branching rightward or downward into outcomes; branch labels at the leaves.
**Tributaries** (convergence over time): several thin streams merging left-to-right into one thick flow; source labels at the left edge.
**Orbit** (one core, dependent satellites): like radial hub but satellites sit *on* a drawn orbit ring, claiming they revolve around — depend on — the core.
**Flywheel** (self-accelerating cycle): ring cycle with progressively thickening segments; use only when the argument is genuinely "momentum compounds."
**Nested shapes** (containment): concentric rounded shapes, labels on each ring's band at 12 o'clock, innermost = most specific.
**Venn / lens** (overlap): two or three circles with real overlap; the shared region labeled — the overlap label is the point of the slide. Use fill transparency so overlaps read.
**Timeline / road** (sequence at scale): horizontal spine with date ticks; events alternating above/below on short stems.
**Chasm** (adoption or capability gap): two landmasses, the gap labeled with what is missing; optionally the bridge variant from the two-point section.

## When the honest answer is a list

If classification lands on "none," set the list beautifully instead: strong short lead-ins in bold, one supporting line each, generous line spacing, a thin rule or ample whitespace between items, no bullets glyphs unless the medium demands them, and absolutely no boxes around individual items. A confident list signals judgment; a forced diagram signals a template.
